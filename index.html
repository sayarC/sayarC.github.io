01 / Product

WallStreetWizard

Streamlit DeepSeek AI Supabase scikit-learn

"As someone who recently started investing seriously, I kept hitting the same wall — the tools built to evaluate stocks assume you already speak the language. So I took a Python project from one of my courses and rebuilt it into the beginner-friendly tool I wished existed."

Problem
New investors want to evaluate options and build toward long-term growth, but existing tools are built for power users — dense dashboards and jargon that turn "let me research this stock" into a chore.
Decisions
Designed three focused flows instead of one overwhelming dashboard: Research Stocks (a plain-English query returns five stocks with charts, stats, and AI opinions drawn from recent news), Stock Analysis (resolves a company to a ticker, then computes stats, a scikit-learn price prediction, a head-to-head comparison chart, and an AI deep dive), and a What-If Simulator (shows what an investment would be worth today, benchmarked against the S&P 500, with AI commentary). Offered two AI model tiers — fast for quick queries, stronger for deep analysis — to balance cost against quality, and had each user supply their own API key so the app stays free to host and scales without absorbing usage costs.
Outcome
A live, multi-user web app with real authentication, AI-driven research, ML price predictions, and S&P 500-benchmarked simulation — shipped end to end and deployed publicly, born from a problem I had myself.

02 / Research & Analysis

Observing AI in the S&P 500

Python pdfplumber statsmodels Panel Regression

Group project · with Doga Gucuoglu & Aishna Gupta
DSO 459: Business Analytics with Python

Problem
Since ChatGPT launched, S&P 500 companies have flooded earnings calls with AI talk — a ~488% jump in mentions. But is that genuine productivity transformation, or just signaling to investors? We set out to test whether companies talking about AI actually got more productive, measured by Revenue Per Employee.
Decisions
Built a pipeline to turn 376 raw earnings-call PDFs into structured data — extracting text with pdfplumber, stripping boilerplate, and scoring each transcript against a 13-term AI keyword dictionary — then merged it with Compustat financials into a 250-observation panel. Ran nine progressively stricter regression models rather than trusting a single result, each designed to rule out a flaw in the last.
Outcome
The naive answer — "AI mentions predict higher productivity" — completely reversed once we controlled for company fixed effects, exposing that sector composition, not AI, drove the surface result. The deeper finding: companies pay a one-time productivity cost when they first commit to AI, and early movers saw ~9.5% higher productivity growth afterward. A reminder that the first answer the data gives you is often the wrong one.
